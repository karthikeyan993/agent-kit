Threat Modeling Session Kit (STRIDE)

Session at a glance

A 90-minute session: 25 minutes of theory, 50 minutes of hands-on STRIDE on the secret rotator, 15 minutes to rate risks and assign owners. Split into two 60-minute sessions if the group is slow or large.

Roles to assign before you start

Facilitator (you)

Keeps time, asks the STRIDE questions, stops the group going down rabbit holes.

Scribe

Writes every threat into the register live. Pick a junior; it is the best way to learn.

System expert

Whoever wrote or knows the rotator. Answers “how does it actually work?”

Participants

Everyone else. Their job is to ask “what could go wrong?”

Prep checklist (day before)

  • Draft the DFD yourself so you are not drawing from zero live (starter below)
  • List the assets: the Okta service account's password (and MFA factor, if any), the 1Password access token the rotator uses, the generated client secrets, the headless browser's session cookies
  • Confirm the setup: headless Playwright on Cloud Run, one dedicated Okta service account, password kept in 1Password
  • Find out who can view the 1Password item and who can run or redeploy the Cloud Run job
  • Prepare a shared threat register (template below)
  • Share the STRIDE cheat sheet in this kit

Ground rules to say out loud

  • No idea is stupid. Juniors often spot what seniors assume away.
  • We find threats now; we do not fix them in the room. Note the fix idea and move on.
  • Focus on design, not code bugs.
  • Time-box: if a debate passes 3 minutes, log it as an open question.
02

What threat modeling is (what to say)

Threat modeling is a structured way to find security problems in a design before attackers find them in production. Say this in the first two minutes; everything else hangs on it.

Script you can read almost word for word

“Threat modeling is thinking like an attacker, on purpose, with a checklist. We take a system, draw how data moves through it, and at every step ask: what could go wrong here? Then we decide what to do about each thing we find. It is cheapest to do this at design time: a design flaw found on a whiteboard costs a conversation; found after a breach, it costs an incident.”

The four questions (the industry-standard core)

Every threat modeling method, including the ones Microsoft and OWASP teach, boils down to four questions from Adam Shostack's framework. Keep them visible all session.

Why we do it (for juniors)

  • Security is a design property. Scanners and pen tests find bugs; they rarely find “we should not have stored that password at all.”
  • It gives the team a shared picture of the system. Many juniors have never seen the whole flow end to end.
  • AppSec and auditors ask for it. It is evidence that risks were considered and accepted on purpose.

When to threat model

  • New system or major feature (this case).
  • A change that touches auth, secrets, trust boundaries, or a new external integration.
  • Whenever the design changes; a threat model is a living document, not a one-time form.
03

Core concepts: the vocabulary

You need five words before STRIDE makes sense: asset, data flow diagram, its four element types, and trust boundary.

Asset — something an attacker wants or something we must protect. For the rotator: the Okta service account's password, the token the rotator uses to read 1Password, the newly generated client secrets, and the headless browser's session cookies. The service account itself is an asset too: it has access to the API platform, 1Password and Zendesk.

Data flow diagram (DFD) — a simple picture of the system using only four shapes. Boxes on a whiteboard are fine.

Trust boundary — a dashed line wherever data crosses between zones with different levels of trust: our runtime ↔ internet, our service ↔ a SaaS vendor, a user ↔ our code. Most real threats sit on these lines. Say: “If you remember one thing, check every arrow that crosses a dashed line.”

Threat vs vulnerability vs risk (juniors mix these up)

Threat

Something bad that could happen. “An attacker steals the Okta password.”

Vulnerability

The weakness that allows it. “The password is visible to anyone with 1Password access.”

Risk

Likelihood × impact. “Critical: one leak gives access to every platform the bot uses.”

Mitigation / control

What reduces the risk. “Limit who can view the item and alert on logins from outside Cloud Run.”

04

STRIDE, letter by letter

STRIDE is six categories of threat, each the opposite of a security property you want. Walk them one at a time across the diagram.

Which letters apply to which element

The classic “STRIDE-per-element” rule. It saves time by telling you which questions to ask where.

ElementSTRIDE
External entity✓✓
Process✓✓✓✓✓✓
Data store✓✓*✓✓
Data flow✓✓✓

* Repudiation applies to data stores that hold logs.

What to say for each letter

S

Spoofing. Can someone act as our rotator, or trick it into talking to a fake service? Example: anyone who can view the bot's password in 1Password can log into Okta as the bot, and every platform will believe it is the rotator.

T

Tampering. Can someone change something in transit or at rest that we trust? Example: someone edits the Playwright script or its npm dependencies, so it also sends the new secret to an attacker's server.

R

Repudiation. If a secret got rotated wrongly at 2 a.m., could we prove whether the rotator did it or a person did? Example: a dedicated account helps, but a human who copied the password from 1Password looks exactly like the bot in Okta and Zendesk logs.

I

Information disclosure. Where could a secret leak? The biggest letter for this app. Examples: the new secret printed in Cloud Run logs, captured in a Playwright screenshot, video or trace, or the 1Password access token sitting in a plain Cloud Run environment variable.

D

Denial of service. What stops rotation from working, and what happens then? Example: Okta changes its login page, the selector breaks, the old secret is already revoked, and the integration goes down. Half-finished rotation is a real outage risk.

E

Elevation of privilege. Does the service account have more power than it needs? Example: it was made only for rotation, but if it is an admin in the API platform or Zendesk, stealing it gives an attacker full admin, not just rotation.

“Spoofing doesn't apply to us, right?” (it does)

Expect this question

A dedicated service account running headless on Cloud Run does not remove spoofing. It changes who could spoof and how. Spoofing asks whether anyone can pretend to be a trusted identity, and this design has three of them.

Identity someone could pretend to beHow, in your designLikelihood
The Okta service accountAnyone who can view the 1Password item can log in as the bot from their own laptopHigh people can view it
The rotator's GCP identityAnyone with permission to run, redeploy or impersonate the Cloud Run job's service account acts as the rotatorMedium depends on IAM
Okta / the platforms, to the rotatorA changed URL in config sends the bot's password to a look-alike pageLow fixed URLs, TLS, headless

What “headless” and “dedicated” actually buy you: no human reads a phishing page (lowers the third row), and logs name one account instead of a shared one (helps Repudiation). They do nothing about the first two rows.

General rule to teach

Never skip a letter silently. If a category truly does not apply to an element, write N/A in the register with one line of reasoning. “Considered and not applicable because…” is a valid result, and AppSec reviewers look for it.

05

Running the exercise

Run STRIDE as a guided game: point at one element or arrow on the diagram, read the questions for one letter, and let the group call out threats while the scribe logs them.

Facilitation steps

  1. Draw the DFD with the group (10 min). Ask the system expert to narrate the flow; you draw. Ask: “What did I miss? Where does this run? Where is the password stored?”
  2. Mark trust boundaries (2 min). Ask: “Where does trust change?” Let juniors try first.
  3. Number every element and flow (1 min). E.g. P1 rotator, DS1 1Password, F1–F6 flows. Threats are logged against these IDs.
  4. Pick the riskiest area first. For this app: the flows that carry secrets across boundaries.
  5. For each letter, ~7 minutes: read the definition, point at the relevant elements, ask 2–3 questions from the bank below, then go round the room.
  6. Log every threat in one sentence using this pattern:An [attacker / failure] could [action] on [element] because [weakness], resulting in [impact].
  7. Park debates. Anything over 3 minutes becomes an open question with an owner.
  8. Rate and propose a mitigation after all six letters, not during.

Techniques that work with juniors

Silent brainstorm first

2 minutes of sticky notes per letter before anyone talks. Stops the loudest person dominating.

“Be the attacker” role

Give one junior the explicit job of attacker each round.

Elevation of Privilege cards

Microsoft's free card deck turns STRIDE into a game. Good for a second session.

Praise thoughtful wrong answers

The goal is to build the habit, not to be complete on day one.

Question bank by letter

S Spoofing
  • Who can view the bot's password in 1Password today? Could any of them log in as the bot?
  • Does Okta enforce MFA or a network restriction for this service account, or is the password alone enough?
  • Who can run, redeploy or impersonate the Cloud Run job's GCP service account?
  • Does the script check it is on the real Okta domain before typing the password?
T Tampering
  • Who can change the script, its config, or the target client ID? Is that change reviewed?
  • Are npm / Playwright dependencies pinned and scanned? What if one is compromised?
  • Could the wrong secret be pasted into the wrong client ID? How do we verify?
  • Is the container image immutable, or can someone edit it in place?
R Repudiation
  • Do we log each rotation: when, which client ID, success or failure, which Cloud Run execution?
  • If a human logs in with the bot's password, can we tell it apart from the rotator (source IP, device, time)?
  • Does 1Password's activity log show who revealed the password, and when?
  • Can someone delete or edit the Cloud Run or rotation logs?
I Information disclosure
  • Where does the new secret exist, even briefly? Browser memory, DOM, screenshot, video, trace, Cloud Run logs, stdout?
  • Are Playwright traces or failure screenshots on? Where are they stored and who can read them?
  • How does the rotator authenticate to 1Password, and where is that token kept?
  • Who can view the 1Password item, and is that list reviewed?
  • Would an error message print the secret?
D Denial of service
  • What happens if a UI changes and a selector breaks halfway through?
  • Is the old secret revoked before the new one is confirmed working? Can we roll back?
  • What if Okta locks the bot account after failed logins, or MFA prompts change?
  • Who gets alerted when a rotation fails, and how fast?
E Elevation of privilege
  • What role does the service account have in the API platform, 1Password and Zendesk? Only what rotation needs?
  • Can the 1Password token read only the one item it needs, or the whole vault?
  • If the service account is compromised, what else can the attacker reach?
  • Does the Cloud Run job's GCP service account have broader access than needed?

How one threat travels from question to ticket

Walk the group through this for T1.

  1. Question asked: “Where does the secret exist, even for a second?”
  2. Answer from the room: “On screen, so in any screenshot or trace.”
  3. Threat statement: “An insider with storage access could read the new secret from a failure trace, because tracing records the page, resulting in a leaked production credential.”
  4. Rating: likelihood High (traces are often on for debugging), impact High → Critical
  5. Response: Mitigate. Ticket: “Disable Playwright tracing on secret pages and mask the secret field.”
  6. Check: rerun a failed rotation and confirm no secret appears in the artifacts.

Questions to settle before the session

  • How does the rotator read the password from 1Password: a 1Password service-account token, Connect server, or something else? Where is that token stored?
  • Does Okta require MFA or a network restriction for the service account? If not, the password alone is the bot's identity.
  • Exactly who can view the 1Password item, and is reveal activity logged?
  • Is the old secret revoked automatically, and when?
  • Are the newly rotated client secrets also saved to 1Password, or only entered into Zendesk?
  • Do the API platform or Zendesk offer an API for this? If yes, browser automation may be the wrong design.
07

Rating risks and choosing mitigations

Keep scoring simple for a junior group: likelihood × impact on a 3-point scale. DREAD and CVSS exist, but they slow a first session down.

Questions to rate quickly

  • Likelihood: “How hard is this to pull off? Does it need insider access, or just a leaked log file?”
  • Impact: “If it happens, what does the attacker get? One secret, or admin on every platform?”

The four responses to a threat

1 · Mitigate

Add a control. The most common response.

2 · Eliminate

Remove the feature that causes it. “Does this platform have an API for rotation? Then we don't need browser automation at all.” Always ask this for the rotator.

3 · Transfer

Make someone else responsible (vendor, insurance). Rare for engineers.

4 · Accept

Document it, with who accepted it and why. Accepted risks need a named owner, usually the product or engineering lead, not a junior.

08

After the session: what AppSec expects

When AppSec says “do threat modeling,” the standard deliverable is a written threat model document, not a video. A recording is optional as an internal reference; it is not evidence AppSec can review.

Industry practice vs your company's call

Known industry practice

A threat model = a diagram + a list of threats + mitigations + risk decisions, kept with the project and updated when the design changes.

Ask your AppSec team

Their exact format isn't known here. Many teams use a template, a tool (Microsoft Threat Modeling Tool, OWASP Threat Dragon, IriusRisk) or a ticket workflow. Ask: “Do you have a template or tool you want this in, and what sign-off do you need?”

Within 2 days after the session

  • Clean up the DFD (draw.io, Threat Dragon, or Miro) and export it as an image
  • Turn the raw list into the threat register below; merge duplicates
  • Create a ticket for every Mitigate item, linked from the register
  • Get named owners to sign off any Accepted risks
  • Send the report to AppSec and attach it to the repo or wiki
  • Set a review trigger: re-run when the flow, platforms, or hosting changes

Report template

1 · Overview
  • System name, owner, version/date, participants and roles
  • Scope: what's in, what's out
  • One paragraph on what the system does
2 · Architecture
  • DFD with numbered elements and trust boundaries
  • Assets and where each one lives
  • Assumptions, e.g. “Runs headless as a Cloud Run job; dedicated Okta service account; its password stored in 1Password”
